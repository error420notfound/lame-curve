01 / Field

The background carries the color.

The page beneath this article is the only part responsible for hue. It is an oversized field of color with a few slow, quiet forms moving through it. The blur layers above are intentionally neutral, so their behavior can be studied without a second palette interfering with the result.

That separation makes the effect easier to read. A colored object becomes a sample. A line of text becomes a measuring instrument. The farther it travels into the stack, the more its edges surrender to the surface around it.

02 / Layer

Depth is a relationship, not a decoration.

When content sits above the stack, every letter remains exact. When it sits inside, the page becomes a gradient of legibility: a little softness at the top, a stronger veil at the bottom. When it sits below, the layers become a quiet boundary between the viewer and the message.

Try each position from the floating controls. The setting changes the stacking order of this entire article, not a screenshot or a fake opacity treatment. Scroll slowly and let the fixed blur field move across the content.

The useful question is not “how much blur?” but “where does the blur begin to belong?”

03 / Motion

Scroll is the simplest test instrument.

A fixed layer stack makes the relationship visible over time. The top of the viewport is close to clear, the lower edge is progressively more obscured, and the content keeps moving behind it. This lets you inspect transition zones instead of judging one isolated blur radius.

Look for the moment that text stops feeling printed on the page and starts feeling embedded in it. That threshold is affected by type size, line height, contrast, background noise and the density of the layers themselves.

04 / Contrast

Light and dark change the material read.

Switching the theme keeps the same progressive radii but changes the neutral veil and the page contrast around it. In light mode, the layers read like a thin, luminous sheet. In dark mode, they become a softer, denser obstruction.

Neither mode is the “correct” one. They are two lighting conditions for the same experiment. The useful comparison is whether the transition still feels continuous, and whether the content remains understandable at the point where you want it to.

A surface can hide information without feeling like a wall.

05 / Texture

Small details reveal the stack.

Thin borders, compact labels and soft color changes are especially good at exposing a progressive blur. Large blocks can remain convincing even when heavily softened. Fine details show where the browser begins sampling the background and how the masks overlap one another.

Use the hue slider to create new test conditions. Keep the content position fixed, then compare a warm field with a cool one. The blur stays neutral; only the page beneath it changes.

06 / Record

Keep the transition quiet.

The best progressive blur does not announce every layer. It should feel like a gradual change in atmosphere: clear enough to invite reading, soft enough to establish depth, and controlled enough that the viewer can still understand what is behind the surface.

This page is deliberately oversized so the effect has room to breathe. Keep scrolling, change the content layer, and use the settings toast whenever you want to return to a known state. Your last mode, layer and hue are saved in this browser.
